Why I Built a 771-Disease Taxonomy Instead of Just Training a Model

📅 July 2026
⏱ 8 min read
🏷 BSc Thesis · Hierarchical ML · NLP

When most people approach a disease prediction problem, they open Kaggle, download a symptoms dataset, and train a Random Forest. You get a model. It works. You move on.

I didn't do that. And this post explains why — and what I found when I went a different way.

The Problem With Flat Classification at Scale

The dataset I was working with had 771 diseases. That's not a typical 10-class classification problem. At that scale, a flat classifier — one that tries to map symptoms directly to a disease — starts to break down in ways that aren't immediately obvious in your metrics.

Consider two diseases: Viral Fever and Dengue Fever. Their symptom profiles overlap significantly. A flat model trained to predict one of 771 labels will often confuse them — not because it's a bad model, but because the task is structurally unfair to it. There's no hierarchy. No concept of "these two are at least in the same family."

A flat model that gets 78% accuracy on 771 diseases sounds impressive. But if it's confusing Dengue with a common cold 15% of the time, it's clinically useless — and dangerously misleading.

Why Davidson's Framework Changed Everything

I found the answer in how doctors actually think. Clinical medicine doesn't work by listing 771 possible diagnoses and picking one. It works top-down: what system is affected? → what category within that system? → what specific condition?

Davidson's Principles and Practice of Medicine organises disease knowledge into body-system-based super-categories. I used this framework to build a three-level taxonomy:

  • Level 1 — Super-Category: 8 groups (e.g. Infectious, Metabolic, Neurological)
  • Level 2 — Category: 14 sub-groups (e.g. Bacterial Infections, Autoimmune Disorders)
  • Level 3 — Disease: 771 individual conditions

Four Architectures, One Benchmark

Once I had the taxonomy, I benchmarked four classification strategies against it:

  1. Flat classifier — baseline, 771-way direct prediction
  2. 2-stage hierarchical — predict category first, then disease
  3. 3-stage hierarchical — predict super-category → category → disease
  4. Adaptive hierarchical transfer — weights shared across levels

The flat model had the best raw accuracy. The 3-stage hierarchical model was my final choice anyway — here's why.

Explainability isn't a feature you add at the end. It has to be baked into the architecture from the start. A 3-stage cascade gives you a natural audit trail: the model failed at the category level, which means the symptom profile was ambiguous at the system level — that's a useful clinical signal.

The Clinical Validation Layer

One thing I wasn't satisfied with in existing symptom-disease datasets was the sourcing. Many are crowdsourced or synthetically generated. A recruiter or clinician looking at DocBot would reasonably ask: where do these symptom-disease relationships come from?

So I built a cross-validation layer: I manually checked 5,372 disease–symptom pairs against two textbook references — Davidson's and another clinical reference text. This wasn't glamorous work. It was slow, careful, and necessary.

It's the kind of thing that doesn't show up in an accuracy metric, but it's what makes a research project credible rather than just performant.

What I'd Do Differently

If I were starting over, I'd invest more time earlier on the symptom normalisation step. "Fever," "high temperature," and "pyrexia" are the same thing — but a naive NLP pipeline treats them as three distinct features. We caught most of these cases, but a more systematic synonym-mapping pass would have cleaned up the feature space considerably.

I'd also push harder on the NLP extraction component — specifically, building a small clinical NLP pipeline to extract structured symptoms from free-text patient descriptions rather than relying on a fixed symptom vocabulary.

Closing Thought

This project took longer than a standard "pick a dataset, train a model" approach would have. It was harder to explain to people unfamiliar with hierarchical classification. And it produced a framework that no existing benchmark was built to evaluate cleanly.

I'd do it the same way again. The extra complexity wasn't overhead — it was the point.
